**Factory has acquired HumanLayer, its third AI coding startup** in less than a month, as it works to fold more of the software engineering lifecycle into a single autonomous platform. The deal deepens Factory’s bench, but it also leaves existing HumanLayer users weighing what to do with the tools they already depend on.

Announced Wednesday, the acquisition brings HumanLayer founders [Dex Horthy](https://www.linkedin.com/in/dexterihorthy/) and [Kyle Mistele](https://www.linkedin.com/in/kyle-mistele/) to Factory, along with technology for coordinating multiple coding agents and collaborating on their work. It arrives as demand for agentic coding tools climbs at a pace [few enterprise products ever see](https://thenewstack.io/gpt-5-6-codex-user-surge), pushing the companies building them to consolidate fast.

> “It arrives as demand for agentic coding tools climbs at a pace few enterprise products ever see…”

Factory co-founder and CEO [Matan Grinberg](https://www.linkedin.com/in/matan-grinberg/) welcomes the pair [on X](https://x.com/matanSF/status/2118069546234077212), writing that he has “been a huge fan of @dexhorthy and @0xblacklight for a while now,” and calling them two of the most ambitious polymaths he has met.

## What happens to users

The founders write in their [announcement](https://www.humanlayer.dev/blog/humanlayer-joins-factory) that Factory will “also be refunding paying customers for the last several months,” though neither company has set a shutdown date for HumanLayer’s hosted service or said which features will carry over.

For users who would rather not migrate, HumanLayer [open-sourced its core software](https://www.humanlayer.dev/blog/open-sourcing-humanlayer) under Apache 2.0 on October 23, an escape hatch that still comes with caveats. Some processes require third-party API keys even when those integrations go unused, a rough edge the company says it is working to remove as it makes the software easier to deploy on its own.

## Three deals, four weeks

HumanLayer caps a quick run of deals that map Factory’s priorities. On October 9, Factory [acquired Mentlio](https://factory.com/news/mentlio), a Y Combinator startup focused on making coding agents more cost-efficient through [model routing, context compression and spending analysis](https://thenewstack.io/minimax-flash-agent-costs/).

Three weeks later, on October 30, it [acquired Typedef](https://factory.com/news/typedef), which builds tools for understanding the relationships across data models, pipelines and dashboards, technology Factory plans to point at enterprise data migrations and refactoring.

HumanLayer brings collaboration to that mix, with multiplayer tasks, shared editing of agent-generated plans and documents, and live streaming of code changes during agent sessions. Factory says those capabilities will begin arriving over the coming weeks without naming specific release dates, and it has not disclosed the purchase price for any of the three deals.

## Inside Factory’s agent playbook

Factory’s coding agents, known as Droids, handle everything from planning and writing code to testing and maintenance. HumanLayer’s founders have focused on what happens when teams run [multiple coding agents at once](https://thenewstack.io/letta-frontiermemory-agent-harness-benchmark/), including managing context, coordinating tasks, and reviewing results.

> Factory’s coding agents, known as Droids, handle everything from planning and writing code to testing and maintenance.

Horthy and Mistele also helped popularize the Research/Plan/Implement workflow, which breaks coding tasks into separate stages. Their work fits Factory’s effort to bring more of the development process into one platform. Mentlio helps control agent costs, Typedef adds enterprise data capabilities, and HumanLayer brings tools for collaboration.

## The standalone question

But HumanLayer users still don’t know whether the hosted service will continue beyond the transition, what they’ll pay if they move to Factory, or which features they’ll be able to keep. The open-source version gives them another option, although they’ll have to host and maintain it themselves. For now, it’s unclear how much of HumanLayer will remain available as a standalone product.

> “HumanLayer users still don’t know whether the hosted service will continue beyond the transition, what they’ll pay if they move to Factory, or which features they’ll be able to keep.”
